# Senior Pet Care

## When is a pet "senior"?

- Small dogs (under 20 lb): senior at 10-12 years
- Medium dogs (20-50 lb): senior at 8-10 years
- Large dogs (50-90 lb): senior at 7-8 years
- Giant breeds (over 90 lb): senior at 5-6 years
- Cats: senior at 10-11 years; "geriatric" at 15+

Larger dogs age faster than smaller ones — a 7-year-old Great Dane and a 12-year-old Chihuahua are at similar life stages.

## Schedule changes for seniors

**Shorter, more frequent walks** for senior dogs. A single long walk can leave them sore for days. Three 15-minute walks beat one 45-minute walk.

**Easier-access litter boxes** for senior cats. Switch from high-sided boxes to low-entry trays. Place a box on every floor of the home if the cat has trouble with stairs.

**More frequent feeding** — senior pets often digest large meals less efficiently. Three smaller meals can work better than two larger ones.

**Vet visits twice per year** instead of annually. Many age-related conditions (kidney disease, diabetes, hyperthyroidism in cats; arthritis, cognitive decline in dogs) progress quickly and benefit from early detection.

## Common senior conditions

**Arthritis**: stiff after rest, reluctant to jump or climb stairs, snapping when touched in painful areas. Joint supplements (glucosamine, omega-3) and prescription pain meds (carprofen, gabapentin) help. Soft bedding and ramps reduce strain.

**Cognitive dysfunction (dog dementia)**: pacing at night, getting "stuck" in corners, forgetting house training, staring at walls. Some prescription diets and supplements can slow progression.

**Kidney disease (especially in cats)**: increased thirst and urination, weight loss, vomiting. A prescription kidney diet is the single most effective intervention.

**Vision and hearing loss**: keep furniture in the same place, approach from the front, don't startle them awake.

## Quality of life

The "5 good things" framework: a senior pet should be able to (1) eat, (2) drink, (3) urinate/defecate without distress, (4) move around without constant pain, (5) engage with the family in some way. When two or more of these are consistently failing, it's time for a vet conversation about quality of life.
